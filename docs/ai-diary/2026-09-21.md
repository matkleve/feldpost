# 2026-09-21

## STUDY-009 finished, and four settings that were scenery

### What shipped

- **#231 — tray question priority.** A static kind→priority table plus one gate,
  `isBudgetSuppressible`. Priority comes from what a wrong answer *costs*, not from how many metres
  the error is: Thalistraße 4 vs 6 and 4 vs 4a are both 20 m apart, and only the second files
  evidence against the wrong client.
- **#232 — the deferred-location backlog**, two figures in the Upload tab, counted from the
  database so they outlive the upload session.
- **#219 — the bulk-resolution confirmation dialog**, which makes those figures clickable. The
  engine had been built and unreachable since 5.3.
- **#229 — the question-per-file curve**, measured across five corpus shapes at three sizes.
- **#230, #237, #238** — four config fields deleted, and a gate so the next one fails the build.

### The measurement killed the feature it was for

STUDY-009 proposed a budget that suppresses low-priority tray questions on large uploads. Measured:
the worst of five shapes asks **29 questions of 10 000 files**, and every one is `critical` —
19 `admin_level_conflict`, 10 `city_step`. Not one `house_step` or `geocode` anywhere in the matrix.

So the budget has nothing to suppress, and #233 closed as not planned rather than parked behind a
threshold. Merge, `area_only` and Issues absorb the volume before a budget could see it: 304 of 383
groups on the worst shape went to Issues without asking anything.

Worth keeping as a pattern: **the study that proposes a feature is also the thing that can retire
it, if it insists on measuring first.** STUDY-009's own § "What is genuinely unknown" is what made
this cheap — the guess was labelled as a guess, so replacing it cost one measurement rather than a
rewrite.

### A predictor is a second implementation, so it needs a pin

Counting questions by kind at 10 000 files meant predicting the kind rather than running the
Angular stack. That is a second implementation of a decision spread across five services. It is
pinned by two specs that run the **real** pipeline over the same corpora and compare kind
histograms — and building against those pins corrected the prediction three times:

- A failed geocode only falls back to a tray on the street-first branches; `street_locality` goes
  partial and asks nothing. Reading a failure as a question gave **58 `city_step` against an actual
  3** — twentyfold, in the direction that makes a budget look necessary.
- `admin_level_conflict` registers per grouping key, not per conflicting field.
- EXIF is not decoration: `Währinger Straße 10` geocodes to one Wien hit and looks resolved until
  the context-distance filter drops it.

Without the pin all three would have shipped as findings. **A measurement harness needs a test that
it measures the thing it claims to.**

### Four settings wired to nothing, and the detection that actually works

`presentationBundleMaxDialogueUnits`, `exifContextCheck`, `clusterAssistWeight`, and nine more in
search tuning: declared, defaulted, documented in a spec, read by nothing.

Grep finds the ones with no reader. It does **not** find the worst kind — a field that has a reader
and is still a duplicate of a live constant somewhere else, which is what #230's was. The detection
that works is **flip the value and re-run**: `exifContextCheck: false` and
`clusterAssistWeight: {0, 0}` gave byte-identical traces (8 reverse geocodes, 62 forward, the same
27 trays), which is proof where a grep is an argument. Promoted into TRAP-010.

The owner's correction, and the right one: *"if code is useless and not in the specs, remove it."*
The first instinct was to park nine search-tuning fields behind a ratchet. Two of them were even in
a spec — which turned out to say *"remain in schema but are runtime no-ops"*. A sentence
documenting that a setting does nothing is not a reason to keep the setting.

**The check to keep from this:** seven of the nine were exact duplicates of values hardcoded in live
code — `countryBoostIn: 1.6` against `return … ? 1.6 : 0.7`, `displayNameTruncateLength: 60` against
`length > 60`. Deleting them changed no behaviour, and confirming that *before* deleting is what
turned a risky cleanup into a safe one.
